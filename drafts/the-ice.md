# The Ice

*Act III. Deep winter, the year after the snowball fight. Aage narrates. Isaac is 12. Ezra is 2 and at home with Miriam's mother.*

---

The river had been frozen nine days. Isaac said ten.

"It's nine," I said.

"It's ten. I counted. You count wrong when you're being stubborn."

We stood on the near bank with the horses ground-tied behind us. Justine, black, patient. Aisha, the chestnut, who hated cold and let everyone know it. On the far side the snare line ran along the tree edge, six wire loops we hadn't checked in four days. Going around by the stone bridge was two hours. Going across was ten minutes.

I put my left boot on the ice and leaned.

"It's making a noise," Isaac said.

"Ice makes noise."

"Not that noise."

I didn't hear it. I'm told I should have. What I felt was the right leg, which feels nothing, and the left, which felt a river sleeping under a lid. I took another step. The lid held. I took six more.

Behind me, boots. He'd come out after me without saying he would. He was ten feet back, arms a little out from his sides, walking the way he walked into the Orchards the first winter, like the ground might be a trick.

"Go back," I said.

"Fuck off."

The sound, when it came, wasn't a crack. It was long, like a plank being drawn slowly across a floor, and it ran under both of us at once.

I turned. That was the mistake, or maybe the leg was. Metal weighs what it weighs. The shelf under me tilted and I went down to the chest in one piece, no splash, no shout, just the world swapping places. Isaac lunged. His hand got my collar. The ice under his knees let go like it had been waiting.

---

Cold is not a temperature. It's a hand closing.

It closed on my left side and squeezed the air out of me in one piece. My right side, the leg and the arm, felt nothing at all, and that was worse: the metal was a stone tied to me, and it did not care whether I lived.

Isaac was beside me. His coat had gone wide around him and it was dragging him. His mouth was open. No sound came out of it.

That was what I looked at. Not the water. His face.

I've seen that look on men in the basement. It's not panic. It's the thing under panic, where a body decides the story is already over and starts putting things away. His eyes were on me and not seeing me.

"Isaac."

Nothing.

I got my left hand into his coat, the hand that can still feel, and I made it close. Then I did the other thing, the thing the arm is for. I put the metal fist under his ribs and drove him up out of the water, onto the shelf, as hard as I'd ever thrown anything.

He hit the ice on his belly and stayed there.

"Flat," I said. "Spread out."

He didn't need telling. His arms were already wide, his legs already apart, his whole body already laid down on the ice like a drawing of a person. Somebody had taught him that once, or he'd taught himself on a winter I wasn't there for.

He crawled. Not toward me. Toward the bank, to the horses, on his stomach, in a coat that was turning to board.

I hung on the edge and counted. I got to forty. I want to say that's strength. It's what happens when the mind goes quiet and the body keeps doing the one thing.

Then the rope came skidding across the ice toward my hand, with a knot at the end that I knew was Isaac's knot, because it was wrong in the same place his bandages always were.

I got it under the metal arm and around my chest. I raised my left hand. On the bank Justine felt the line go tight and leaned into it the way she'd leaned into everything for years.

---

I don't remember the bank. I remember pine needles under my face and Isaac's hands on me, small and shaking, tearing at the buckles of the coat.

"Get it off," he said.

"I'm all right."

"You're *blue.* You're blue, you stupid fucking... get it off. Everything wet. *Now.*"

The metal fingers had frozen to the glove. He worked it loose with a knife, careful, the way he'd carried the horses' feet when he was small, and set the glove on a rock. The arm underneath was white with frost to the elbow, and the join at my shoulder had gone gray.

I got the coat off. Then the boots, which took both of us. His fingers were already starting to fumble. I watched him try to work the second one and saw him fight for the grip.

Then I looked down at my own hands. The left one was steady. The right one was a metal fist and nothing else.

"Your shirt," I said.

He'd started to move toward the saddlebags. He stopped.

"Off."

"No."

"Isaac—"

"I said no." His voice was tight, pitched higher than it should be. "You're not—I'm not—no."

He walked to where the fire would be and started to gather wood. His hands were shaking too much to hold it. The branches fell. He picked them up. They fell again.

"I'll help you get the fire—" I started.

"I don't need help. I'm fine. It's one fucking river, Aage, it's not—"

His hands wouldn't close. He looked down at them like they'd betrayed him, and his jaw locked.

I got to my feet. The wet wool of my own shirt stuck to my ribs, and the cold was already starting to settle in for the long work of eating me. I took the branches from him. Set them down. Then I looked at him straight.

"You're ten minutes from not being able to move your fingers at all," I said. "After that it's the arms. Then the legs. Then you're dead in a ditch and Ezra grows up explaining why his brother was too ashamed to take his shirt off."

His face went scarlet. Not from the cold.

"Fuck you," he whispered.

"Probably. But that's not right now. Right now you need to get dry."

"I'll dry off by the fire."

"No, you won't. You'll freeze into that shirt and seize up and I'll have to strip you anyway while you're screaming. That sound better?"

He looked away. His jaw was working. I could see him thinking through the options the way he thought through traps: which one least destroys him.

"Turn around," he said finally. His voice was so quiet I almost didn't hear it.

"No."

His eyes snapped back to me, wide and furious.

"Look at me," I said. "Only at my face. I need you to get warm. I don't care about anything else. Turn around, get the shirt off, wrap up. That's it. Nothing you do is shameful. You're alive."

He turned around. I turned around. For a moment neither of us moved.

Then wet fabric hit the ground.

My own shirt followed. The cold hit like a wall the second the air touched me, so complete it felt like the river had come back for me. I wrapped the first blanket around my chest twice, sealed the metal arm in a fold so it wouldn't leach any more heat into the rest of me, and kept my back to him.

Behind me, another blanket shifted. He was wound into it like a cocoon, only the top of his head visible. Even from the back, I could see his shoulders shaking.

"Nobody speaks of this," he said. "To anyone."

"I know."

"Hadi finds out, I kill him."

"All right."

"Com finds out, I burn down the chapel."

"I believe you."

"If Ezra—"

"He won't know. I'll take it to my grave."

He was quiet for a long moment. Then: "You'd better."

We built the fire together, mostly me working with my left hand while he managed the flint. It took six tries. Each time it dropped, he went very still, and I didn't say anything. On the seventh try it caught. He nursed the flame like it was his only child, coaxing it higher, feeding it twigs and then branches until there was real heat.

Then he sat down wrapped in the blanket like a burrito, only his furious face showing, and wouldn't look at me.

"Don't," he said, before I could say anything.

"I wasn't going to."

"Yes, you were. I can hear you thinking about saying something."

"You're right. I was going to say that you're alive because you're stubborn as stone."

He glared at the fire instead of answering.

"And that you got me out of water that was trying to kill me."

Still nothing.

"And that twelve-year-olds are allowed to be afraid of things."

His hands came up. He wrapped them around his mouth inside the blanket cocoon. When I realized he was crying, I turned away and kept my eyes on the fire the way he'd asked me to keep my eyes on his face.

We sat like that for a while. The fire grew. The cold started to become something we could fight instead of something that would just win.

"Don't you seize on me," he said, not looking up. "Don't you fucking dare."

"I'm not."

"You're shaking."

"It's the cold."

"It's the thing before the thing."

He was right. I felt it, the old pressure behind the eyes. Cold, no sleep, a shock. I put my head down and breathed the way Com taught me, in for four, out for six, and the pressure thought about it and went away.

He watched me the whole time.

---

We had the rock lip over us and the fire in front and one blanket each, then one blanket between us, because the second one couldn't hold the heat. Our clothes hung from a branch like men who'd been hanged. The horses stood with coats over their backs, blowing steam.

Isaac sat with his knees up, wrapped to the chin. He wasn't talking. His right hand lay in his lap with the nails of the left dug into the scar on his wrist.

I'd have said something once. I'd have been wrong. I put my flesh hand over his, over the scar, not holding, just covering, and waited.

He let it stay.

"Hadi would've floated," he said after a while. "He's all fat. He'd have bobbed along down to the sea."

"He'd have had opinions about it."

"He'd have *yelled* about it."

A pause. The fire popped.

"I did this once," he said.

"I know."

"You don't."

"No," I said.

He was quiet long enough that I thought that was the end. Then, to the fire, very flat: "Nobody pulled me out."

I didn't say anything. There wasn't anything that wouldn't have been a lie or a lesson.

"I got out," he said. "That's it. That's the whole thing. I got out, and it was cold."

"Okay."

"Okay."

He put his head down on his knees. I kept my hand where it was. At some point his breathing changed, and then his weight leaned, not all at once, a few degrees at a time, the way a wall settles. He was asleep against my shoulder, and I didn't move, because the arm that would have moved was frozen and the other one was holding the only thing in the world I'd never let go of.

---

Morning came in blue. The clothes on the branch had frozen into planks. The horses stood with frost on their lashes, bored with us.

Isaac woke with one fist wound into the edge of my blanket and a look on his face like he'd been caught stealing. He sat up so fast his teeth clicked, the chipped one catching on the one beside it.

"I didn't—"

"No."

"I was cold."

"Yes."

He looked at the fire, which was out. At the branch. At his frozen shirt.

"Ezra's going to ask why you smell like smoke."

"Tell him I fought a bear."

"I'm telling him you fell in the river like an idiot."

"That's accurate."

I got the blanket around me and found my coat. It had thawed a little, enough to wear. I held it out to him without a word.

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he took it. It went on over his head and the sleeves hung past his hands and the hem fell nearly to his knees. He pushed his fists up inside the cuffs and stood there, a small shape in a big man's coat, with his chin tucked into the collar.

We went home by the bridge. Two hours. He walked beside me the whole way in my coat, sleeves past his hands, and didn't say one word about it.
