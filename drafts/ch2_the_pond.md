# Chapter 2: The Pond

I was reading when the house went silent in that particular way that meant Isaac had gone outside.

The book in my lap was the same one I'd been trying to get through for three days. I turned pages without absorbing them, the words sliding past my eyes like water. It was past dark. The lamp beside me cast my shadow long across the wall. Outside, the night was complete—the kind of dark that only comes when you're far enough from other people, other lights.

I listened. This was something I'd gotten good at over the years—listening to the absence of sound, hearing what was missing. The absence of Isaac moving through the house. The absence of him in the other room, in the kitchen, climbing the stairs. The soft sound of a door opening. The near-silent closing of it.

He was gone.

I didn't move immediately. Let him have this. Let him have the moment of leaving, the belief that I didn't know, the privacy of his own desperation. That was something I could give him, at least. The mercy of thinking he was alone with it.

I kept reading the same page. The words still didn't land. The story was about people in a city I'd never been to, problems that seemed very small and very far away. Outside, Isaac was walking toward the pond.

This was what we did. This was the language we'd developed—not in words, but in movement. In the knowing without saying. When it got too big, when the pressure inside him got too high and there was nowhere for it to go, he left the house and went to water. And I waited long enough for him to believe I was staying, and then I followed, because the other thing he needed to know—the thing I had to show him by moving, by following—was that I didn't stay because I was trapped. I stayed because I chose to.

I set the book down on the small table beside the lamp. It slumped there, unfinished, one page dog-eared. I didn't want to dog-ear pages. It felt like violence against something that had already suffered. But sometimes you do violence to things. Sometimes things require it.

The night air was cool when I stepped outside. There was a lamp hung by the door—oil, old, the kind that had survived wars and weather. I took it down and lit it, the flame catching and steadying. The light pushed back the dark about six feet in all directions, and beyond that was nothing.

I knew the path. I'd walked it enough times—following Isaac on nights like this, letting him move ahead until he thought he was truly alone, then appearing like something that had been summoned. Not appearing. That suggested something magical, something less deliberate than what I was doing. I was simply doing what I'd promised, wordlessly, by showing up. By being there.

The path went through the trees and down toward the water. I could hear it before I could see it—the small sounds of a pond at night, the shift of things moving in it, the gentle lap of water. And underneath that, I could hear something else: the sound of movement, of clothes being undone, of the specific sound a boy makes when his body is in distress and he's trying to release it into something larger than himself.

I slowed. I let him get into the water first. That was the kindness I could offer—a few moments alone in it, a few moments before I appeared and proved that he wasn't alone after all, that this thing he needed couldn't scare me away.

The water was dark, the kind of black that means it's cold. I could see his shape in it, a boy's shape, pale and angular in the light. He was up to his waist, moving deeper, and his movements had the quality of someone both desperate and grateful—like the water itself was answering a question he'd been asking.

I set the lamp down on the rocks. I stepped out of my clothes without ceremony. I did it the way I did everything else—with the absolute lack of shame that comes from having nothing left to hide. What was there to be shy about? He'd already seen all of me. He already knew exactly what I was broken about.

The water was shockingly cold. It took my breath, the kind of cold that makes you understand why people go to water in the first place. It wasn't gentle. It wasn't a comfort. It was something more honest than that—it was real, and it didn't pretend to be anything else.

Isaac heard me. He turned, the water moving around him. For a moment, he just looked at me, his face in shadow, unreadable. I couldn't tell if this was what he wanted or if I'd made another mistake, if my following had been the wrong choice again.

Then he moved toward me, and I understood: this was right. This was the thing he needed. Not words. Not explanations. Just the knowledge that when he left, when he did the thing he had to do, I would still know where he was. I would still come.

He came into my arms and I held him. The water was cold around us both, but his skin was warm, still feverish from the heat of his anger and his storm. He was shaking.

"I'm sorry," he said into my chest. His voice was small. It was a child's voice. It was the voice of a boy who had been terrified that he'd broken the most important thing in his world.

I pressed my face into his wet hair. "No," I said. "No, Isaac. I'm here. I'm still here."

He held on tighter, and we stood in the cold water, in the dark, in the place where he'd come to be alone and found instead that he never had to be.
